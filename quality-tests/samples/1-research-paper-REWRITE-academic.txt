The Effects of Remote Work on Employee Productivity and Well-Being
Author Name
Institutional Affiliation

Abstract
The rapid expansion of remote work has generated scholarly debate about its impact on employee outcomes. This study examined how remote work affects two core constructs: productivity and psychological well-being. An online survey collected responses from 312 full-time employees in diverse industries who had been working remotely for at least three months. Productivity was assessed with the Individual Work Performance Questionnaire (IWPQ; Koopmans et al., 2014) and well-being with the WHO-5 Well-Being Index (Topp et al., 2015). Results showed a moderate level of self-reported productivity (M = 3.78, SD = 0.62 on a 5-point scale) and relatively high well-being (M = 71.4, SD = 13.2 on a 0–100 scale). Pearson correlations revealed a positive association between productivity and well-being (r = .34, p < .001). Comparing full-time remote workers with hybrid workers (≥2 days in office) using an independent-samples t-test indicated that hybrid employees reported higher productivity (M = 4.02 vs. 3.61; t(310) = 4.87, p < .001) and higher well-being (M = 75.6 vs. 68.9; t(310) = 3.21, p = .001). The findings suggest that remote work can maintain productivity, while a blended arrangement may improve both performance and mental health.

Keywords: remote work, productivity, employee well-being, hybrid work, telecommuting

Introduction
The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of remote work, turning it from a niche arrangement into a mainstream employment model (Brynjolfsson et al., 2020). While supporters claim that remote work provides flexibility, lessens commuting stress, and can increase productivity (Bloom et al., 2015), opponents caution that it may foster isolation, blur work–life boundaries, and lead to lower performance (Wang et al., 2021). Assessing the overall impact of remote work on employee outcomes therefore remains essential for managers shaping post-pandemic work policies.

Productivity denotes the amount and quality of work output (Koopmans et al., 2014), while well-being refers to subjective psychological health, including affective balance and life satisfaction (World Health Organization, 2015). Research shows that remote workers can surpass office-based peers in some contexts (Choudhury, Foroughi, & Larson, 2020) but may also experience lower engagement and greater burnout in others (Gajendran & Harrison, 2007; Oakman, Kinsman, Stuckey, Graham, & Weale, 2020), and the distinction between full-time remote and hybrid arrangements appears to moderate these outcomes. Consequently, productivity and well-being remain interrelated yet distinct.

The present study aims to (1) quantify self-reported productivity and well-being among remote employees, (2) examine the relationship between these constructs, and (3) compare outcomes for full-time remote and hybrid work configurations. Using a large, cross-industry sample and rigorous statistical analyses, the research provides empirical clarity to the ongoing debate.

Literature Review
Remote Work and Productivity. Early meta-analyses found a modest positive effect of telecommuting on performance (Gajendran & Harrison, 2007). Bloom et al. (2015) reported a 13% productivity increase among Chinese call-center agents after they began remote work, linking the rise to quieter environments and fewer breaks. However, productivity may decline over time. Wang et al. (2021) observed that extended remote work reduced productivity because of home distractions and less supervision. These mixed results could reflect variation in job type, autonomy, and support structures.

Remote Work and Well-Being. Remote work offers flexibility that may improve work-life balance and increase life satisfaction (Allen, Golden, & Shockley, 2015). Isolation and diminished social interaction can, however, raise stress levels and symptoms of depression (Mann & Holdsworth, 2003). Research from the pandemic-era depicts this arrangement as a double-edged sword: although many employees report less commuting stress, a significant minority experience increased anxiety and burnout (Oakman et al., 2020).

Hybrid Models as a Potential Moderator. Hybrid work, which combines remote days with on-site presence, represents a compromise. By examining employees with occasional office visits, Choudhury et al. (2020) determined that these individuals maintained higher collaboration quality while retaining flexibility. Such hybrid arrangements may preserve productivity and mitigate loneliness according to preliminary evidence (Kreiner, Hollensbe, & Sheep, 2022). Systematic comparisons between full-time remote and hybrid outcomes remain scarce, however.

Research Gaps. Most existing studies rely on single-company samples or pre-pandemic data, limiting their generalizability (Kniffin et al., 2021). Few investigations simultaneously assess productivity and well-being, which hinders understanding of their interdependence in remote contexts. This study addresses these gaps through a multi-industry survey with robust statistical testing.

Methods

Design and Participants
The study utilized a cross-sectional, online survey design. Participants were recruited through professional networking platforms, including LinkedIn and professional forums, from June to August 2024. Inclusion criteria required individuals to be at least 18 years of age, employed in full-time positions of 35 hours per week or more, engaged in remote work for at least three consecutive months, and fluent in English. A total of N = 312 respondents completed the questionnaire, with 58% identifying as female and a mean age of 38.2 years (SD = 9.4). The sample included employees from technology (34%), finance (22%), education (15%), healthcare (12%), and other sectors (17%).

Measures
- Remote Work Arrangement: Participants indicated whether they worked fully remote (100% of workdays at home) or hybrid (2 or more days per week in the office).
- Productivity: The IWPQ short form (Koopmans et al., 2014) consists of 8 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1, strongly disagree, to 5, strongly agree. This measure attained a Cronbach’s α = .89 in the present sample.
- Well-Being: The WHO-5 Well-Being Index (Topp et al., 2015) has 5 items. Responses range from 0 (at no time) to 5 (all of the time). Raw scores are multiplied by 4 to obtain a scale of 0 to 100. Cronbach’s α = .92.

Procedure
Participants provided informed consent before completing demographic questions, the IWPQ, and the WHO-5. The survey, hosted on Qualtrics, required approximately 12 minutes to finish. Researchers screened data for incomplete responses (n = 7) and outliers exceeding 3 SD from the mean, which they then winsorized.

Statistical Analyses
The study calculated means and standard deviations for productivity and well-being. Pearson’s correlation assessed the linear relationship between these variables. To compare mean scores between full-time remote and hybrid groups, the researchers performed independent-samples t-tests. Effect sizes appear as Cohen’s d. Analysts used SPSS v.28 for all procedures, setting α = .05 for significance testing.

Results

Descriptive Statistics
- Productivity: M = 3.78, SD = 0.62 (scale 1–5).
- Well-Being: M = 71.4, SD = 13.2 (scale 0–100).

Correlation
Productivity and well-being were positively correlated, r = .34, p < .001. This result suggests that higher self-rated performance aligns with greater psychological well-being.

Hybrid workers reported significantly higher levels of productivity and well-being. The effect size for productivity, d = 0.71, indicates a medium-large difference. By contrast, the well-being effect, d = 0.49, represents a medium difference.

Discussion

The findings illuminate the nuanced impact of remote work on employee outcomes. Overall, participants reported moderate to high productivity and well-being, aligning with prior evidence that remote work does not inherently diminish performance (Bloom et al., 2015). The positive correlation between productivity and well-being corroborates the “happy-productive worker” hypothesis (Wright, 2007), suggesting that psychological health may reinforce work output in remote settings.

Hybrid Advantage
Hybrid workers achieved higher productivity and well-being scores than those working entirely remotely. Occasional in-person interaction appears to improve collaboration, social support, and organizational identification, which explains why such contact improves performance and mental health (Kreiner et al., 2022). A medium-large effect size for productivity suggests that for many knowledge-based jobs, the advantages of face-to-face contact surpass the flexibility gained through full remote work.

Implications for Practice
Organizations ought to adopt hybrid arrangements as a standard practice rather than an outlier. Policies granting employees the autonomy to select a balanced distribution of remote and office work may maximize productivity while protecting well-being. Because gaps persist for fully remote staff, firms should also provide virtual socialization tools and ergonomic support to address these disparities.

Limitations and Future Research
The cross-sectional design of this research precludes causal inference. Future longitudinal studies should examine how productivity and well-being evolve as remote arrangements persist. Because self-report measures may be subject to social desirability bias, researchers should incorporate objective performance metrics in subsequent work. Investigating moderating variables such as job autonomy, managerial support, and home environment quality would also refine the current understanding of who thrives under remote versus hybrid conditions.

Conclusion

The evidence suggests that remote work, especially when combined with periodic office attendance, maintains or improves employee productivity and well-being. Full-time remote work remains a viable option for many individuals, yet hybrid arrangements produce superior outcomes across the measured dimensions. Organizations operating in a post-pandemic environment may therefore benefit from adopting flexible and balanced work structures to support a productive and healthy workforce.

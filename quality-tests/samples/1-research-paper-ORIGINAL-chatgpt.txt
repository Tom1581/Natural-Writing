The Effects of Remote Work on Employee Productivity and Well‑Being

Institutional Affiliation

Abstract
The rapid expansion of remote work has sparked scholarly debate about its impact on employee outcomes. This study investigates how remote work influences two central constructs: productivity and psychological well‑being. An online survey was administered to 312 full‑time employees from diverse industries who had been working remotely for at least three months. Productivity was measured with the Individual Work Performance Questionnaire (IWPQ; Koopmans et al., 2014) and well‑being with the WHO‑5 Well‑Being Index (Topp et al., 2015). Descriptive statistics revealed a moderate level of self‑reported productivity (M = 3.78, SD = 0.62 on a 5‑point scale) and relatively high well‑being (M = 71.4, SD = 13.2 on a 0–100 scale). Pearson correlations indicated a positive association between productivity and well‑being (r = .34, p < .001). An independent‑samples t‑test comparing employees who worked remotely full‑time versus hybrid (≥2 days in office) showed that hybrid workers reported higher productivity (M = 4.02 vs. 3.61; t(310) = 4.87, p < .001) and well‑being (M = 75.6 vs. 68.9; t(310) = 3.21, p = .001). Findings suggest that while remote work can sustain productivity, a blended arrangement may enhance both performance and mental health. Implications for organizational policy and future research directions are discussed.

Keywords: remote work, productivity, employee well‑being, hybrid work, telecommuting

Introduction
The COVID‑19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of remote work, transforming it from a niche arrangement to a mainstream employment model (Brynjolfsson et al., 2020). Proponents argue that remote work offers flexibility, reduces commuting stress, and can boost productivity (Bloom et al., 2015). Critics, however, warn of isolation, blurred work–life boundaries, and potential declines in performance (Wang et al., 2021). Understanding the net effect of remote work on employee outcomes is crucial for managers designing post‑pandemic work policies.

Productivity and well‑being are interrelated yet distinct outcomes. Productivity refers to the quantity and quality of work output (Koopmans et al., 2014), whereas well‑being encompasses subjective psychological health, including affective balance and life satisfaction (World Health Organization, 2015). Prior research reports mixed results: some studies find remote workers outperform office‑based peers (Choudhury, Foroughi, & Larson, 2020), while others note reduced engagement and higher burnout (Gajendran & Harrison, 2007; Oakman, Kinsman, Stuckey, Graham, & Weale, 2020). Moreover, the heterogeneity of remote arrangements (full‑time remote vs. hybrid) may moderate these effects.

The present study aims to (1) quantify self‑reported productivity and well‑being among remote employees, (2) examine the relationship between the two constructs, and (3) compare outcomes across full‑time remote and hybrid work configurations. By employing a large, cross‑industry sample and rigorous statistical analyses, this research contributes empirical clarity to an ongoing debate.

Literature Review
Remote Work and Productivity. Early meta‑analyses identified a modest positive effect of telecommuting on performance (Gajendran & Harrison, 2007). Bloom et al. (2015) demonstrated a 13 % productivity increase among Chinese call‑center agents after shifting to remote work, attributing gains to quieter environments and fewer breaks. Conversely, Wang et al. (2021) reported that prolonged remote work can erode productivity due to distractions at home and reduced supervision. The inconsistency may stem from differences in job type, autonomy, and support structures.

Remote Work and Well‑Being. The flexibility of remote work can improve work‑life balance, leading to higher life satisfaction (Allen, Golden, & Shockley, 2015). However, isolation and lack of social interaction can elevate stress and depressive symptoms (Mann & Holdsworth, 2003). Recent pandemic‑era studies highlight a “double‑edged sword”: while many employees report reduced commuting stress, a sizable minority experience heightened anxiety and burnout (Oakman et al., 2020).

Hybrid Models as a Potential Moderator. Hybrid work—combining remote days with on‑site presence—has emerged as a compromise. Choudhury et al. (2020) found that employees with occasional office visits maintained higher collaboration quality without sacrificing flexibility. Preliminary evidence suggests hybrid arrangements may preserve productivity while mitigating loneliness (Kreiner, Hollensbe, & Sheep, 2022). Yet, systematic comparisons of full‑time remote versus hybrid outcomes remain scarce.

Research Gaps. Most existing studies rely on single‑company samples or pre‑pandemic data, limiting generalizability (Kniffin et al., 2021). Moreover, few investigations simultaneously assess productivity and well‑being, hindering understanding of their interdependence in remote contexts. The current study addresses these gaps through a multi‑industry survey with robust statistical testing.

Methods

Design and Participants
A cross‑sectional, online survey design was employed. Participants were recruited via professional networking platforms (LinkedIn, professional forums) between June and August 2024. Inclusion criteria: (a) age ≥ 18, (b) full‑time employment (≥35 h/week), (c) remote work for a minimum of three consecutive months, and (d) fluency in English. A total of N = 312 respondents completed the questionnaire (58 % female; mean age = 38.2 years, SD = 9.4). The sample comprised employees from technology (34 %), finance (22 %), education (15 %), healthcare (12 %), and other sectors (17 %).

Measures
- Remote Work Arrangement: Participants indicated whether they worked fully remote (100 % of workdays at home) or hybrid (≥2 days per week in the office).
- Productivity: The Individual Work Performance Questionnaire (IWPQ) short form (Koopmans et al., 2014) includes 8 items rated on a 5‑point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Cronbach’s α = .89 in the present sample.
- Well‑Being: The WHO‑5 Well‑Being Index (Topp et al., 2015) consists of 5 items scored from 0 (at no time) to 5 (all of the time); raw scores are multiplied by 4 to yield a 0–100 scale. Cronbach’s α = .92.

Procedure
After providing informed consent, participants completed the demographic items, followed by the IWPQ and WHO‑5. The survey was hosted on Qualtrics and took approximately 12 minutes. Data were screened for incomplete responses (n = 7) and outliers (> 3 SD from the mean) which were winsorized.

Statistical Analyses
Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations) were calculated for productivity and well‑being. Pearson’s correlation examined the linear relationship between the two variables. Independent‑samples t‑tests compared means between full‑time remote and hybrid groups. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d. All analyses were conducted in SPSS v.28, with α = .05 for significance testing.

Results

Descriptive Statistics
- Productivity: M = 3.78, SD = 0.62 (scale 1–5).
- Well‑Being: M = 71.4, SD = 13.2 (scale 0–100).

Correlation
Productivity and well‑being were positively correlated, r = .34, p < .001, indicating that higher self‑rated performance aligns with greater psychological well‑being.

Both productivity and well‑being scores were significantly higher for hybrid workers. The effect size for productivity (d = 0.71) suggests a medium‑large difference, whereas the well‑being effect (d = 0.49) is medium.

Discussion

The findings illuminate the nuanced impact of remote work on employee outcomes. Overall, participants reported moderate to high productivity and well‑being, aligning with prior evidence that remote work does not inherently diminish performance (Bloom et al., 2015). The positive correlation between productivity and well‑being corroborates the “happy‑productive worker” hypothesis (Wright, 2007), suggesting that psychological health may reinforce work output in remote settings.

Hybrid Advantage
Hybrid workers outperformed fully remote peers on both dimensions. This supports the notion that occasional in‑person interaction can bolster collaboration, social support, and organizational identification, thereby enhancing performance and mental health (Kreiner et al., 2022). The medium‑large effect for productivity indicates that the benefits of face‑to‑face contact may outweigh the flexibility gains of full remote work for many knowledge‑based jobs.

Implications for Practice
Organizations should consider hybrid models as a default rather than an exception. Policies that allow employees to choose a balanced mix of remote and office days could maximize productivity while safeguarding well‑being. Additionally, firms should invest in virtual socialization tools and ergonomic support for fully remote staff to mitigate the observed gaps.

Limitations and Future Research
The cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference; longitudinal studies could examine how productivity and well‑being evolve as remote arrangements persist. Self‑report measures may be subject to social desirability bias; future work could incorporate objective performance metrics. Finally, exploring moderating variables such as job autonomy, managerial support, and home environment quality would refine understanding of who thrives under remote versus hybrid conditions.

Conclusion

This study provides empirical evidence that remote work, particularly when blended with periodic office attendance, sustains or enhances employee productivity and well‑being. While full‑time remote work remains viable for many, hybrid arrangements appear to deliver superior outcomes across the measured dimensions. As organizations navigate the post‑pandemic landscape, adopting flexible yet balanced work structures may be key to fostering a productive and healthy workforce.
